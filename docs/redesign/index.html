A photographer’s site, set like a sports page.

The recommendation in one paragraph: a quiet grey ground tuned for white ice, a newspaper serif for the voice and a newsroom grotesk for captions, galleries sequenced by hand with real datelines, four links in the navigation, and a static site built from photos in the repository. Hockey leads. Street work gets its own slower page. Aviation and wildlife sit in a smaller third room. The R2 catalog pipeline, slideshow, lightbox and upload-portal plans go away.

1. Project assessmentWhat exists today and what should change

The site has a careful image pipeline and almost no design.

The repository is a static site on GitHub Pages that renders everything in the browser from catalog.json. A GitHub Action scans a Cloudflare R2 bucket every six hours, makes WebP thumbnails and resized copies with Pillow, writes the catalog, generates album pages, a sitemap and an Atom feed, and commits the result. The engineering is disciplined. The architecture review from August found the weak points: of roughly 1,083 commits, about 1,033 are the bot rewriting a timestamp, no photo was added after 20 June, no album has a date or location, and images come off the rate-limited r2.dev endpoint with no edge cache.

The live homepage shows the cost. A slideshow of 23 images (aviation, ducks, Golden Gate, hockey) sits behind a white headline with a drop shadow. Below it, two of the three category tiles render blank because their covers are missing, and the “Latest Albums” strip sorts on empty dates. Space Grotesk carries every line of type. Nothing on the first screen says sports is the focus. An editor arriving from an introduction sees a Thunderbirds formation and a generic hero.

2. The photographsAll 54 images in the R2 bucket, reviewed before any design decision

Two bodies of work, one strong and one stronger than it looks.

Hockey: 20 frames

Contact sheet of the hockey/select-images album, in catalog order.

  • 18 frames come from San Jose State club games (blue and gold), several against Santa Clara, most of them at SAP Center. Sony A7R IV, 70–200mm f/2.8, 1/500 to 1/1000 s, ISO 640 to 1600. Two frames are from a Sharks game in the stands, on a Fujifilm X100VI.
  • Ice fills 40 to 60 percent of most frames. The pictures are high-key, cool white with saturated royal blue and gold. On a pure white page the ice bleeds into the background and the photograph loses its edge. This single fact drives the choice of background colour.
  • Strongest frames: the puck in the air between shooter and goalie (04395), the clean slapshot follow-through (04674), the Santa Clara player looking away while San Jose State celebrates behind him (04314), the goal embrace (05875), and the arena from the upper deck (DSCF0958).
  • Weak spots an editor will notice: the same San Jose State No. 21 appears in five frames, sponsor boards dominate several backgrounds, and there are no goaltender close-ups, faceoffs, bench or tunnel moments, warm-ups, or tight peak-action crops. The set reads as two or three games from one program. Section 13 lists what to shoot next.

Street and places: about 20 frames, mostly Leica Q3

  • This is a consistent voice: single figures against architecture, silhouettes, motion blur on trains, night signage, a few black-and-white frames. San Francisco, Seattle, Chicago, New York, and a platform that looks like the London Underground (to confirm).
  • The black-and-white frame of a fan filming a hockey game from the stands (L1000452) belongs to both bodies of work. It becomes the last plate of the hockey sequence and the hinge between the two galleries.
  • Travel work overlaps with street. The Golden Gate frames are well made but familiar. There is not enough distinct travel work for its own section.

Aviation, wildlife, coast: about 12 frames

Good craft, small numbers. Enough for one modest page, too few for separate Aviation and Wildlife sections.

3. Visual research14 sites captured at 1440 and 390 pixels, with inner gallery pages where they mattered

What the good ones do, specifically.

Each site was loaded in a real browser at desktop and phone widths and scrolled through. The notes say what each one does, so the lessons transfer. Four of the most useful photographer sites (Iooss, Schutmaat, Finke, Powell) run on Squarespace with the template stripped down, which matters for the platform decision in section 10.

Walter Iooss sports

Every photo carries a caption in one fixed form: subject, place, year (“Willie Mays, Milwaukee, WI, 1966”). That consistency is what makes a sports archive look professional. Portfolios are split by sport. The condensed logotype IOOSS is the only loud element.

Borrow: the caption discipline. Avoid: the uniform three-column stagger for a 12-image sequence.

Bryan Schutmaat documentary

One image per screen, about 40 percent of the width, with large equal gaps. Projects are indexed by roman numerals I to VIII. The Information page is a plain list: contact, galleries, books, clients, education. Garamond at small sizes, nothing else.

Borrow: the slow pacing for Street, the facts list for About. Avoid: images this small for sports action.

Brian Finke sports as documentary

Images at three or four different sizes, offset left and right, so the page has a rhythm without a visible grid. Cheerleaders and football players treated as documentary subjects. Captions sit tight under the photo in small type.

Borrow: scale changes and offsets placed on purpose. This is the closest model for the hockey page.

Victory Journal sports publication

Sport written about with literary seriousness. A serif text face (Hoefler Text) on a light grey ground (#F3F3F3), not white, so photographs sit like prints. Story pages open on one full-width picture, then a 60-character text column.

Borrow: serif for sport, grey ground. Avoid: the newsletter pop-up and display type laid over the photo.

Racquet sports culture magazine

Tennis as culture: PT Serif headlines, warm off-white ground, art-led imagery. Proof that a sports site does not need to look like a broadcaster.

Avoid: commerce chrome (Subscribe, Log in, pill buttons) crowding the masthead.

Mundial football magazine

A strong condensed display face and a boxed grid. Identity is clear, but the homepage is a shop. It shows the sports-merchandise register Neil’s site should stay away from.

Rinko Kawauchi photographer

The ground is a pale blue-grey (#EAEEEE) chosen to suit her pale, luminous pictures. Navigation is a short serif list in a fixed left column. One image fills the rest.

Borrow: tint the neutral to the work. Here that means a grey that makes ice read as white.

Matt Stuart street

Brilliant pictures in a uniform two-column grid of equal crops. Every frame gets the same size, so none of them leads. On a phone they shrink to stamps.

Lesson: the uniform grid is the thing to avoid for Street.

Gus Powell street

A loose wall of photos, book covers and video at mixed scale. Personality comes from sequencing and humour. Navigation is almost absent.

Lesson: mixed scale works when the order is intentional. Random scale reads as a mood board.

Joel Meyerowitz street

A left column with fifteen navigation items next to a single image. Shows how a navigation list grows when every project becomes a link.

Lesson: keep the navigation to four words.

Alec Soth photographer

Opens with “My name is Alec Soth (rhymes with ‘both’). I live in Minnesota.” A plain sentence in his own voice does more than any tagline.

Borrow: the plain, factual introduction.

Aperture photography publisher

Custom serif and sans pair with a clear ladder: section label, headline, standfirst. Small sans labels identify the kind of story without shouting.

Borrow: the two-family hierarchy. Avoid: the institutional density.

Blind Magazine counter-example

“UNSEEN STORIES.” in heavy Inter with an orange full stop, a grey subtitle, then blocks titled “Cultural Shift” and “Modern Mindset.” A photography magazine whose homepage has no photograph. This is the generated-template look the brief warns about.

Phone layouts across the set

Iooss and Finke keep two columns on a phone, which shrinks action photos too far. Schutmaat and Victory Journal go to one column with generous gaps and hold up best. Every reference site hides its navigation behind a menu icon on phones, even with four or fewer links.

Also attempted but blocked for automated capture: Simon Bruty, Brad Mangin, Stephen Shore, Todd Hido, Gregory Halpern, and Cargo’s template gallery. They are not used as evidence here. Established NHL photographers (Bruce Bennett, Dave Sandford) publish through Getty and the Hockey Hall of Fame rather than personal sites, so there is no hockey photographer’s personal site to imitate. That is an opening for Neil.

4. Three art directionsDifferent structures, not different colour schemes

Wire, Plate, Night Game.

Each direction starts from a different idea of who opens the link. Wire is for the photo desk. Plate is for someone who loves photobooks. Night Game is for a fan.

A. Wire

Credible and efficient. Cold. Treats street work as coverage.

Thesis

The site behaves like a photo desk’s selects. Every image numbered, datelined and captioned to the wire standard, a filmstrip of the take beneath the lead image. It answers the editor’s questions before they ask.

Typography

Schibsted Grotesk for everything: 600 for the wordmark and datelines, 400 for captions and body, tabular figures for plate numbers and times. One family, the way a wire service runs one family.

Colour

#FFFFFF ground
#F2F2F2 alt
#0E0E0E text
#5A5A5A secondary
#DADADA rules

No accent. Secondary text 6.9:1 on white.

Layout

Strict 12 columns. Image always cols 1–8, caption block cols 9–12, filmstrip below. Mobile stacks image, caption, strip. EXIF visible.

Homepage and portfolio

Homepage is the hockey lead image plus a strip of 8 frames. Galleries repeat the same lead-plus-caption unit for every image.

Impression

Getty’s editorial browser, a newspaper photo desk. Sits alongside AP Images and a sports wire.

Why not

Every image gets the same treatment, which flattens sequence. Pure white makes ice bleed. The chinatown walker captioned like a news event is absurd. It reads as a stringer’s archive, and Neil wants to read as a photographer.

5. RecommendationOne direction, built from two

Dateline: Plate’s pacing, Wire’s captions.

Dateline takes the photobook’s respect for sequence, scale and white space from Plate, and the photo desk’s discipline from Wire: every hockey picture says who, where and when, in a consistent form an editor recognises from Getty and AP. Night Game contributes nothing except a warning about type over images.

The combination fixes each parent’s failure. Plate was too slow and too coy for editors, so Dateline uses large images on the hockey page, puts the word Hockey first in the navigation, and shows teams and venues in captions. Wire flattened every image to the same unit, so Dateline varies scale and position by hand, image by image. The result is credible to a media relations director and still feels like a photographer made it.

The bold move is the caption system, and it is functional. Plate numbers in tabular figures, a short factual line, and the dateline in semibold. Nobody else in the reference set does this for personal work, and it tells an editor in one glance that Neil knows how photographs get filed.

6. Design systemSmall enough to fit on one page

Two typefaces, six colours, five row types.

Typefaces

Newsreader (Production Type, 2020, SIL Open Font License, Google Fonts) carries the voice: introductions, titles, the About text. It was designed for reading news on screens and has an optical-size axis from 6 to 72, so the 72px gallery title is drawn with finer contrast than the 19px body text. It looks like a newspaper without looking like Times.

Schibsted Grotesk (Bakken & Bæck for Schibsted, the Norwegian newspaper group, OFL, Google Fonts) does the working jobs: wordmark, navigation, captions, plate numbers, footer. It is a newsroom grotesk with tabular figures (tnum, checked in the font file) and a slightly narrow build that suits long captions.

Both are variable fonts. Self-host Latin subsets as WOFF2: Newsreader roman about 75 KB, Schibsted about 40 KB. Preload the Schibsted file (it renders the header) and Newsreader roman. Load Newsreader italic only on About. No all-caps anywhere on the site.

Paper is a neutral grey with a faint green bias, the colour of matte photo paper or rink boards under arena light. Against it, ice reads as white and keeps its edge. It is cooler and darker than the cream grounds common on generated portfolios, on purpose. Blueline takes its name from the painted lines on a rink and appears only where something is interactive. The photographs supply all the other colour.

No dark mode. The photographs are judged against one ground, and a second ground would change how every picture reads. The page sets color-scheme: light.

Spacing scale (px): 4 · 8 · 12 · 16 · 24 · 32 · 48 · 72 · 112 · 168 · 240. Hockey rows are 112 apart on desktop and 72 on phones. Street rows are 168 and 96, slower on purpose. Homepage sections are 168 and 96. Header to first content: 72 desktop, 32 phone.

Image sizing: never cropped, never rounded, never shadowed, never bordered. No image is taller than the viewport: max-height: 88vh on desktop, width follows the ratio. Every image has width and height attributes so the layout does not move while loading. Placeholder ground is #D6D8D4 or, better, the photo’s average colour computed at build time.

Components

Header
Not sticky; photographs get the whole viewport. Desktop: 84px tall, wordmark from column 1, navigation starting at column 5 so it lines up with the left edge of the homepage’s opening photograph. Phone: wordmark on line one, the four links on line two, about 96px total. No hamburger, because four short words fit at 360px.
Wordmark
Type only: “Neil Kodner” in Schibsted 600 with “Photographer” in 400 Ink 2. No logo, no monogram. The favicon is “NK” in Schibsted 600, Ink on Paper.
Lede
Homepage only. Newsreader 34px, beside the opening photograph and aligned to its bottom edge.
Gallery header
H1 at columns 1–5, standfirst at columns 7–11 aligned to the H1’s baseline, then one small Schibsted line: “12 photographs · Index · Contact for assignments”.
Plate
One figure: picture element, plate number, caption. Anchor id is the number (#7) so anyone can link to a single frame.
Row types
Full (cols 1–12) · Wide (cols 1–9 or 4–12, caption in the remaining 3 columns, bottom-aligned) · Upright (a vertical in cols 5–8 or 8–11, caption beside it) · Pair (two photos at equal height across cols 1–12, one shared caption, only for images from one moment) · Coda (cols 3–10, last plate). Section 9 shows each.
Index
End of each gallery: every plate as a 96px-tall thumbnail at its true ratio, in a single wrapping row, numbered, linking to its anchor. The only place thumbnails appear.
Assignment line
Paper 2 band at the end of Hockey and on the homepage: Newsreader 30px, “For assignments and editorial work” and the email address as a large link. A copy-address button sits beside it.
Footer
Paper 2, 13px Schibsted, three groups: name and location; email and LinkedIn; “© 2026 Neil Kodner. Photographs may not be reproduced without permission.”

Interaction

  • Text links: 1px underline in Ink 2, offset 3px. Hover: underline turns Blueline and 2px, text turns Blueline. No transition longer than 120ms.
  • Focus: 2px solid Blueline outline, offset 3px, on every link and button, including index thumbnails.
  • Linked images (homepage teasers, index): no zoom, no dimming. The caption link underlines on hover of the figure.
  • Anchor jumps scroll smoothly only when reduced motion is off. scroll-margin-top: 24px.
  • No scroll animations, no fades on load, no parallax, no lightbox, no carousel. The site reads the same with JavaScript off. The single script copies the email address.

The navigation says “Hockey” instead of “Sports” because hockey is all the sports work there is. Calling it Sports promises range the portfolio does not have yet. When Neil adds another sport, the link becomes “Sports” and Hockey becomes the first sequence inside it. A section named “Work” would hide the one word an editor scans for.

Travel does not get a section. Its best pictures are street pictures and go there. Aviation and wildlife go to Elsewhere, outside the main navigation, which keeps the hierarchy honest: hockey first, street second, other interests present but smaller.

9. Photography presentationSequence, row types, captions, files

A working sequence for Hockey.

Built from the 20 frames in R2 to prove the system. Neil makes the final selection. The logic: open on the clearest single moment, drop to one player, widen to the crowd at the net, show one goal from both sides, step back to the building, return to individuals, celebrate, breathe, go to the NHL, and end on the audience.

Street order

01 Chinatown, San Francisco · 02 pier silhouette · 03 Seattle ferry passenger · 04 Oculus, New York (vertical) · 05 Bay Bridge cyclist (black and white) · 06 Wrigley Field · 07–08 CTA and Underground platforms (pair) · 09 Chicago Theatre and manhole · 10 bagel baker, New York · 11 Pike Place Market · 12 red sculpture at night · 13 silhouettes at sunset. The order alternates bright and dark, colour and black and white, and never puts two black-and-white frames side by side except as a pair.

Rules for any gallery

  • Never crop to fit a layout. The layout adapts to the ratio. Crops happen in Lightroom, by Neil, for the picture’s sake.
  • Open on a horizontal (desktop) and name a vertical alternate for phones.
  • Change scale at least every second plate. No more than two Full rows in a row.
  • Pair only frames from the same moment or a deliberate rhyme. Never pair to fill space.
  • Twelve is the target for Hockey. Ten strong plates beat fourteen with filler.

Captions

Hockey captions follow the editorial order of who, what, where, when, in two parts: a plain sentence, then the dateline in semibold. Use team names and numbers, add player names only when Neil knows them for certain. No shutter speeds or focal lengths on the page; editors who want EXIF ask for files. Street and Elsewhere captions give place and year only.

Image handling

Lightroom export
JPEG, sRGB (not Display P3: sRGB displays the same everywhere and survives conversion), 3000px long edge, quality 88, output sharpening Screen / Standard, metadata Copyright and Contact only, Remove Location Info on.
Masters
Stored in the repository under src/photos/hockey/ etc. About 1.5 MB each, so 35 images is about 55 MB.
Derivatives
Generated at build: widths 480, 800, 1200, 1600, 2200, 3000. AVIF at quality 60 and JPEG (mozjpeg) at quality 80 as fallback. No WebP: AVIF covers current browsers and JPEG covers the rest.
sizes attribute
Full: (min-width:1100px) min(1520px, 91vw), 100vw. Wide: (min-width:1100px) 68vw, 100vw. Upright: (min-width:1100px) 30vw, (min-width:700px) 60vw, 92vw. Pair: computed per image from its share of the row.
Loading
Plate 01 and the homepage opener: loading="eager" fetchpriority="high". All others: loading="lazy" decoding="async". Width and height on every image.
Alt text
Required in the content file; the build fails without it. Describes the picture plainly: “A San Jose State player in blue shoots; the puck is in the air on its way to the Santa Clara goaltender.” Captions do not repeat as alt text.
Social previews
Each page’s Open Graph image is its first plate, cropped to 1200 × 630 at build. This is the one place a crop is allowed.
Privacy
Build fails if any master contains GPS EXIF. Derivatives carry no metadata.
10. Technical recommendationThe simplest build that produces correct responsive images

Eleventy, photos in the repo, GitHub Pages.

Eleventy 3 with the official @11ty/eleventy-img plugin, Nunjucks templates, one YAML file per gallery, one CSS file, about 20 lines of JavaScript for the copy button. GitHub Actions builds on every push to main and deploys to GitHub Pages. The custom domain and DNS stay as they are.

OptionVerdictReason
Hand-written static HTMLNo35 images × 6 widths × 2 formats of srcset markup by hand will drift. The derivatives need a tool regardless.
Eleventy + eleventy-imgYesOutputs plain HTML, no client framework. The image plugin is maintained by the Eleventy team and handles AVIF, JPEG, widths, sizes and caching.
AstroNoGood image tooling, but components, Vite and a framework upgrade cycle for a site with no interactivity.
Next.jsNoAn application framework. Nothing here is an application.
Squarespace (a stripped template like Schutmaat or Iooss)Credible fallbackFour strong references run on it. Costs about $200 a year, no code. Its galleries cannot do captions beside images, true-height pairs or per-plate phone behaviour without hand-placing every block, and Neil gives up the stack he already runs.
CargoNoDesigner-friendly, but layouts are composed by hand per page and the image pipeline is opaque.
Keep R2 for imagesNoThe bucket needs a nameserver move to get CDN caching. GitHub Pages already serves from a CDN. 55 MB of masters in git is fine at this curation pace.

Publishing workflow

  1. Export from Lightroom with the preset above into src/photos/hockey/.
  2. Add or reorder the entry in hockey.yml: file, row, alt, caption, dateline.
  3. Preview with npx @11ty/eleventy --serve (optional).
  4. Commit and push. The site is live in about two minutes.

Running cost: $0 beyond the domain. GitHub Pages’ soft limit of 100 GB a month covers roughly 30,000 views of the full hockey page.

14. Design quality checklistPass or fail, before launch

Criteria for the finished site.

  • A stranger can say “sports photographer, hockey” within five seconds of opening the homepage on a phone.
  • The word Hockey is the first navigation item and appears above the fold at 390px.
  • The email address is reachable in one tap from every page and appears in full on Hockey.
  • No photograph is cropped, rounded, bordered, shadowed, filtered or overlaid with text.
  • No image exceeds the viewport height on any tested device.
  • Every hockey plate has a caption with a dateline. Every image has alt text.
  • Scale changes at least every second plate on Hockey. No two Full rows back to back.
  • Only two typefaces load. Total font weight under 130 KB.
  • No uppercase text anywhere. No icons. No pill, chip or badge shapes.
  • Lighthouse (phone) on Hockey: Performance ≥ 95, Accessibility 100, Best Practices 100, SEO 100. LCP under 2.0 s on a 4G profile. CLS 0.
  • All text meets WCAG 2.2 AA. Focus is visible on every interactive element. Tab order follows reading order.
  • The site is complete with JavaScript disabled.
  • Shared links show the right image and title in iMessage, Slack and LinkedIn.
  • Updating a gallery requires editing one YAML file and adding JPEGs. Nothing else.
  • Every statement about Neil on the site is one he wrote or approved.
15. Design reviewThe anti-template test, answered honestly

What changed after the review.

The first draft of this direction used a warm cream ground (#F1EFEA), a serif display face and a brick-red accent. That combination is one of the most common looks in generated portfolio and editorial sites. It was replaced with the cooler grey Paper and the Blueline accent, which also serve the ice better. Two other things were cut: a homepage “contents” block that duplicated the navigation, and a lightbox the large images made unnecessary.

QuestionAnswer
Could this be mistaken for a generated site?The risk sits in the serif-on-light-ground family. It is answered by specifics a template lacks: the grey tuned to ice, newsroom captions with datelines, hand-assigned row types, no hero text over images, no cards, no capitals, no icons.
Does it rely on landing-page patterns?No centred headline, no button, no feature cards, no testimonials. The only call to action is an email address in a sentence.
Does the typography feel chosen?Both faces come from news publishing, which is the world an editor works in. Each has one job.
Do the photographs lead?The first screen is 60 percent photograph on desktop and 70 percent on a phone. Type never sits on an image.
Is the homepage interesting without decoration?Its interest comes from composition: text bottom-aligned to a photograph, mirrored previews, a change of scale at Elsewhere.
Does the gallery feel curated?Each plate has a stated reason for its position (section 9). Pairs exist only where frames share a moment.
Memorable?The datelined captions and the grey ground are the signature. Quietly specific, not loud.
Credible to an editor or media relations director?Hockey first, teams and venues named, an index, the email in full. It looks like someone who knows how pictures get filed.
Broader identity?Street has its own slower page with equal craft. Elsewhere shows range at a smaller scale.
Will it hold up in five years?Nothing in it is fashionable. Newsreader, a grey ground and a 12-column grid aged well in print for decades.
Is every component justified?Header, plate, index, assignment line, footer. Five components. The copy button is the only script.
Could something simpler work?Plate alone is simpler and fails editors. Removing captions would remove the main credibility signal. This is the smallest version that does both jobs.
Anything added because sites are expected to have it?Dark mode, a lightbox, a newsletter, a feed, a blog, a hamburger menu and scroll animations were each considered and left out.
