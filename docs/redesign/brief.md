# Project: Redesign NeilKodner.com from First Principles

*The brief Neil wrote for the November 2026 redesign. The proposal it produced is [`index.html`](index.html) in this folder.*

---

You are acting as a senior creative director, art director, editorial designer, UX designer, and experienced web design lead. You have a strong understanding of photography portfolios, editorial publications, independent creative professionals, and modern web design.

Your assignment is to rethink and redesign NeilKodner.com.

I want a genuinely excellent photography website. It should feel considered, professional, contemporary, personal, and visually confident. It should look like the work of a talented human designer who understands photography and editorial design.

I am open to starting completely from scratch. You have permission to recommend replacing the existing design, frontend, framework, architecture, content structure, and visual system. Keep existing components or infrastructure only when they offer a clear practical advantage.

I am asking you to make strong creative decisions. Do not simply produce a safe collection of contemporary web design conventions. I want a distinctive, coherent design with a clear point of view.

The most important part of this project is the visual design. Technology and architecture should support the design and remain simple to maintain.

## 1. Understand the photographer and the purpose of the site

Neil Kodner is an independent photographer based in Los Gatos, California, in the San Francisco Bay Area.

Photography is a serious, long-standing pursuit. His work includes:

* Sports photography, with a particular focus on hockey
* Street and documentary photography
* Travel, architecture, and places
* Aviation, wildlife, and other personal photographic interests

Sports photography is the area where Neil wants to develop more professional opportunities. He has extensive experience around hockey and wants to build on that background through editorial assignments, media relationships, team access, and professional sports photography.

Sports should therefore have a prominent position on the website, with hockey as the strongest portfolio area.

The broader identity matters too. Neil considers himself a photographer with a range of interests and a particular enthusiasm for sports. The site should support that identity over time.

The positioning to communicate is simple: Neil is a photographer. Sports and hockey are his primary professional direction.

Do not turn the website into a sports photography agency, youth sports photo sales operation, or generic photography business. It should feel like the personal website of an independent photographer with a serious photographic practice and a clear professional direction.

### Primary objectives

1. Present a compelling body of photographic work.
2. Make the hockey and sports work easy for editors, team media relations staff, publishers, and potential clients to find.
3. Establish credibility as a photographer.
4. Present the broader range of photography in a cohesive way.
5. Make it easy for someone to get in touch.
6. Create a permanent online home for the work that Neil owns and controls.

The website should be useful when someone receives a link to it after an introduction, credential request, or conversation about an assignment.

It should communicate confidence and professionalism without feeling self-important or overproduced.

Do not invent clients, publication credits, awards, credentials, assignments, testimonials, or professional relationships. Where information is missing, identify what would be useful to add and leave it for Neil to supply.

## 2. The central design challenge

I want a clean, modern, sophisticated photography website that avoids the familiar visual language of AI-generated websites.

I am particularly concerned about the increasingly recognizable look of websites generated by Claude and similar AI coding tools. They often look polished at a glance, but many share the same visual patterns. I do not want those patterns to define this website.

I am completely comfortable with established design conventions. A conventional editorial grid, restrained navigation, a simple portfolio layout, or a well-designed starter theme can be excellent.

Conventional is fine. Generic is not.

The website should have a recognizable design sensibility of its own. It should achieve that through art direction, typography, composition, image pacing, and careful detail.

Do not attempt to make the site look distinctive by adding unnecessary decoration, unusual interactions, or visual gimmicks.

### Explicitly avoid these common AI-generated design patterns

Treat the following as warning signs. Do not use them by default.

#### Generic layout and composition

* The oversized centered headline followed by a short subtitle and a large rounded call-to-action button.
* The standard SaaS landing-page structure with a hero, three feature cards, a benefits section, testimonials, and another call-to-action.
* Repetitive three-column card grids used simply because they are easy to generate.
* Every section having the same width, alignment, spacing, and visual structure.
* Excessively symmetrical layouts that make the site feel assembled from a template.
* Large empty hero areas that contain very little useful information.
* Repeated rows of rounded containers holding otherwise ordinary content.
* A homepage that resembles a startup product page.

#### Generic styling

* Purple, blue, or multicolor gradient backgrounds without a genuine design reason.
* Gradient text.
* Glowing accents, blurred color blobs, and decorative background shapes.
* Glassmorphism and translucent floating panels.
* Excessive rounded corners on images, content areas, cards, and buttons.
* Soft shadows applied to everything.
* Pill-shaped badges and decorative label chips.
* Generic icon grids and line icons used as decoration.
* Arbitrary borders, rules, circles, and geometric shapes.
* Overuse of bold display typography simply to make the design seem modern.

#### Generic typography

* Defaulting to Inter, system sans-serif, or another familiar web font without considering alternatives.
* Using oversized typography everywhere.
* Using several typefaces without a coherent typographic system.
* Using all-caps labels everywhere to imitate editorial design.
* Treating font selection as an afterthought.

#### Generic photography presentation

* Uniform thumbnail grids that make every photograph feel interchangeable.
* Random masonry layouts with no attention to visual rhythm.
* Excessive framing, borders, shadows, and rounded corners around photographs.
* Cropping photographs to fit a rigid component system.
* Treating every image as a thumbnail inside a card.
* Using stock photographs or AI-generated photography to fill gaps.
* Adding ornamental effects that compete with the photographs.

#### Generic interaction and motion

* Scroll-triggered animations on nearly every section.
* Parallax effects without a clear purpose.
* Animated text reveals, floating elements, and unnecessary transitions.
* Custom cursors or other novelty interactions.
* Motion that makes the website feel like a design portfolio demonstrating frontend tricks.

#### Generic language and branding

* Empty phrases such as "Capturing moments that matter."
* Generic positioning statements such as "Where stories meet the lens."
* Inflated marketing copy.
* Fake social proof, invented statistics, fabricated testimonials, or decorative client logos.
* Overly polished copy that sounds like a branding agency wrote it.

This list is not a prohibition on every individual technique. A technique can be used when it serves the design. The point is to avoid automatically assembling the familiar AI-generated visual vocabulary.

Before recommending any design element, ask yourself whether it genuinely improves the experience or simply makes the page look more like a contemporary website template.

## 3. The visual direction I want you to pursue

Think about the intersection of:

* Contemporary editorial design
* Independent photography publications
* Excellent photographer portfolios
* Art books and photography annuals
* Modern cultural magazines
* Minimal, typography-led studio websites
* Thoughtful portfolio design from independent creative professionals

The site should feel at home in the world of photography and visual culture.

It should be image-led. The photographs need room to breathe and should be the most important elements on the page.

The design should have restraint. Every element should earn its place.

I want the result to feel:

* Professional
* Editorial
* Modern
* Calm
* Confident
* Personal
* Photographic
* Carefully composed
* Easy to navigate
* Memorable without being theatrical

Avoid making it feel like a luxury real estate website, wedding photographer template, commercial agency site, sports marketing company, design studio portfolio, or tech startup.

### Design references and creative research

Conduct meaningful visual research before settling on a direction.

Look at actual websites. Do not rely exclusively on lists of recommended sites, screenshots from inspiration galleries, or design trend articles.

Research a selection of:

1. Excellent independent photographer portfolios.
2. Established sports and hockey photographers.
3. Documentary and street photographers.
4. Contemporary editorial publications and magazines.
5. Well-designed independent creative studios.
6. High-quality portfolio templates or starter themes that could serve as a foundation.

Aim for at least 12 useful references across these areas.

For each reference, consider:

* Typography and type hierarchy
* Navigation and header design
* How the homepage introduces the photographer
* How photographs are sequenced and presented
* Image size and aspect ratio
* Grid composition
* Use of whitespace
* Color and background treatment
* Page transitions and navigation
* Mobile presentation
* Contact and biography presentation

Identify the specific design decisions that make the strongest examples work.

Do not simply say that a website is minimalist, modern, or elegant. Explain what it actually does well.

Include links to useful references.

### Explore established templates as legitimate options

I am comfortable using a known template, theme, or starter if it provides an excellent foundation.

Research relevant options before recommending a fully custom design. Consider whether an established portfolio framework could provide a better visual result with less implementation and maintenance work.

A template is acceptable when:

* Its underlying layout is genuinely well designed.
* Its typography and image presentation are strong.
* It can be customized without fighting its assumptions.
* It does not force a generic visual identity.
* It keeps the site simple to maintain.

Do not reject a template just because it is a template. Equally, do not select one merely because it is convenient to implement.

## 4. Develop an actual art direction

This is a critical part of the assignment.

Before recommending a framework or discussing implementation details, develop three meaningfully different visual directions for the website.

These should be real design concepts, not three slightly different color palettes applied to the same generic layout.

For each direction, define:

### A. Design thesis

Give the direction a short name and describe its visual personality.

Explain why it is appropriate for Neil's photography and professional objectives.

### B. Typography

Recommend actual typefaces for:

* Primary display typography
* Body copy
* Navigation and utility text
* Captions and image metadata

Explain how they work together.

Consider serif, sans-serif, and editorial combinations. Look beyond the usual default web fonts. Check actual availability, licensing, performance, and practical implementation.

Do not choose a typeface simply because it is fashionable. Typography should contribute to the site's identity and remain comfortable to read.

### C. Color

Define a restrained color palette with actual hexadecimal values.

Consider:

* Main background
* Alternate background
* Primary text
* Secondary text
* Accent color
* Borders and subtle interface elements

The photography should determine how much visual color the interface needs.

Avoid palettes that compete with the photographs.

### D. Layout and composition

Define:

* Header and navigation treatment
* Content width
* Grid system
* Image presentation
* Section spacing
* Alignment and composition
* Desktop and mobile behavior

Explain where asymmetry, varied image proportions, or changes in scale could add visual interest.

### E. Homepage concept

Explain what the visitor sees first and how the page progresses.

Describe the relationship between the introductory typography and the photographs.

### F. Portfolio concept

Explain how the galleries will present images, how sequences will feel, and how the layout will adapt to different image orientations.

### G. Overall impression

Describe the emotional response the design should create.

Give examples of the kind of photography publication, magazine, or independent creative practice it could sit alongside.

After evaluating all three directions, choose the strongest one.

I want you to make the recommendation. Do not leave me with three equally weighted options and ask me to choose.

Explain why your recommended direction has the best combination of visual quality, professional credibility, longevity, and maintainability.

If you believe the strongest direction combines aspects of two concepts, explain how you would combine them.

## 5. Photography is the design material

This is a photography website. The design must be developed around the images.

Do not treat photographs as content that will simply be inserted into a finished website template.

The photography should influence the layout, proportions, pacing, visual hierarchy, and color decisions.

Neil will supply and curate the final photographs. Do not invent a portfolio or assume that you can select final images before seeing them.

There may be existing image files or photography assets in the repository. Inspect what is available and make recommendations based on the actual material.

For the initial hockey portfolio, assume approximately 12 carefully selected photographs. The final selection will be made by Neil.

Think carefully about:

* A strong opening photograph
* Image sequencing
* Alternating wide and vertical photographs
* Full-width images where appropriate
* Paired photographs where they genuinely belong together
* Intentional whitespace between images
* How photographs interact with surrounding typography
* Whether captions add value
* Whether image metadata should appear
* How a gallery feels when viewed from beginning to end

Do not force every photograph into an identical thumbnail component.

Do not use random masonry as a substitute for art direction.

The gallery should feel curated. It should have rhythm and a clear visual flow.

Pay particular attention to mobile. A composition that works beautifully on desktop can become awkward when reduced to a narrow column.

### Image treatment

Develop a practical image strategy that considers:

* Original aspect ratios
* Consistent color and display behavior
* High-resolution screens
* Responsive image sizes
* Image loading and performance
* Lazy loading
* Appropriate image formats
* Sharpness and compression
* Alt text
* Optional captions and image metadata

Avoid image treatments that unnecessarily alter the photographs.

## 6. Identity and information architecture

The site should establish a broad photographic identity while giving sports and hockey the strongest professional emphasis.

Do not make every subject area equally prominent simply for the sake of symmetry.

The hierarchy should reflect Neil's interests and goals.

Potential areas of photography include:

* Sports, particularly hockey
* Street and documentary
* Travel and places
* Aviation and wildlife, potentially as additional work or future sections

These are possible content areas, not a requirement to launch with a large number of pages.

Recommend a simple navigation structure.

Consider whether the site needs separate navigation links for every genre or whether a compact structure such as Work, About, and Contact could be more effective.

You should make this decision based on the actual amount and quality of work available.

The initial site should likely include:

### Homepage

A concise introduction to Neil as a photographer.

The homepage should give sports and hockey a prominent visual presence while communicating the broader range of his work.

Do not turn it into a conventional marketing landing page.

Let the photographs and typography establish the identity.

### Sports / Hockey portfolio

This is the most important professional portfolio area.

It should be especially strong when shared with:

* Sports editors
* Editorial photo desks

* Publications
* Potential assignment clients

It should demonstrate the quality, consistency, and range of Neil's sports photography.

### Travel portfolio

A curated selection of travel and place photography, if there is enough strong work to justify a dedicated section.

### About

A short, natural introduction to Neil and his photography.

Avoid an unnecessarily long professional biography or generic personal branding language.

### Contact

A clear, professional contact page with a direct email link and any relevant social links.

## 10. Technical philosophy

I am comfortable going scorched earth on the existing implementation.

Do not let the current architecture dictate the design.

Inspect the existing repository, website, documentation, infrastructure, and Git history. Understand what exists before recommending what to keep.

The existing project may contain:

* Custom image processing
* Cloudflare storage or delivery infrastructure
* Catalog or manifest generation
* Custom upload tooling
* Deployment automation
* Existing frontend components
* Previous implementation plans
* Architectural documentation

Evaluate these independently.

Keep something only when it offers a clear benefit relative to its complexity.

I want a small, dependable website with a simple publishing workflow.

I am happy to publish photographs manually.

A reasonable workflow could be:

1. Export appropriately sized JPEGs from Lightroom.
2. Add the images to the project or its image storage.
3. Update a simple content file if needed.
4. Deploy the site.

The workflow should be easy to understand and repeat.

Avoid building infrastructure for hypothetical future needs.

Unless there is a compelling reason, I do not need:

* A custom content management system
* A custom upload portal
* A database
* User authentication
* Client galleries
* Booking or payment functionality
* Complex album management
* Multi-level categories
* Search and filtering
* A generalized photo publishing platform
* A complicated administrative interface

Recommend the simplest reliable technical approach that supports the design.

You may evaluate Astro, Eleventy, Next.js, static HTML, or another appropriate solution. Do not choose a framework simply because it is popular.

A static site is perfectly acceptable.

The resulting site should be easy to maintain, fast, accessible, and inexpensive to operate.

## 14. Final design review: the anti-AI-slop test

Before presenting the final recommendation, evaluate the proposed design critically.

Ask yourself:

* Could this design be mistaken for a generic Claude-generated website?
* Does it rely on familiar AI-generated landing-page patterns?
* Does the typography feel chosen or simply defaulted?
* Do the photographs lead the composition?
* Is the homepage visually interesting without being decorative?
* Does the gallery feel curated?
* Does the design have enough personality to be memorable?
* Does it feel credible to an editor or sports media professional?
* Does it communicate a broader photographic identity?
* Will the design still feel good in five years?
* Is every component justified?
* Could a simpler design achieve the same result?
* Does any part feel like it was added because modern websites are expected to have it?

Be honest. If the design feels generic, revise it before presenting it.

A successful result should look like a thoughtfully designed independent photographer's website, with the visual intelligence of a good editorial publication and the restraint of a confident portfolio.

It should feel distinctive because of the quality of its decisions, not because it is trying to look different.
